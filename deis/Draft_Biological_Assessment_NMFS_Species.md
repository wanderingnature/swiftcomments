# INTRODUCTION

The U.S. Department of Agriculture proposes to rescind the 2001 Roadless Area Conservation Rule (2001 Roadless Rule) (66 FR 3244, 36 CFR Subpart B (2001)), which prohibits road construction, road reconstruction, and timber harvesting in inventoried roadless areas on National Forest System lands, with limited exceptions. Rescinding the 2001 Roadless Rule is intended to return decision making for these activities to local officials, in conjunction with Forest-level land management planning. The 2001 Roadless Rule applies to approximately 44.7 million acres of National Forest System lands, including in Alaska.

Accompanying the Proposed Rule, which is published in the Federal Register, and an environmental impact statement, the Forest Service has prepared this draft ‘framework programmatic’ biological assessment in compliance with the Endangered Species Act. This draft biological assessment discloses the potential effects to ESA-listed resources under National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, National Marine Fisheries Services (NFMS) jurisdiction that would result from the proposed 2001 Roadless Rule rescission. An additional biological assessment has been prepared to assess effects to ESA-listed resources under Department of the Interior, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) jurisdiction. The Forest Service is requesting framework consultation and conferencing and commits to consulting on any future projects in the action area that may affect ESA-listed species or designated critical habitats.

All documents, including the proposed rule and other supporting information, may be found on the following website: https://www.fs.usda.gov/managing-land/planning/roadless.

## Roadless Rule History

Throughout its history, the Forest Service has managed undeveloped areas to conserve their unique characteristics. After passage of the Wilderness Act of 1964 (Pub. L. 88-577), management of these areas, commonly known as “roadless areas,” became the focus of national attention when the Forest Service initiated a review to determine their suitability for inclusion in the National Wilderness Preservation System, known as the Roadless Area Review and Evaluation (RARE) I (1973) and RARE II (1977). In the decades following the inventory, roadless area management continued to be controversial in land management planning and project proposals to harvest timber, build roads, or otherwise develop these areas. In 1999, President Clinton directed the Forest Service to undertake a public process to “provide appropriate long-term protection for most or all of these currently inventoried ‘roadless’ areas...”, initiating the rule-making process. In 2001, the Forest Service issued the Roadless Area Conservation Rule establishing a national-level rule to maintain the values of inventoried roadless areas, limit their development, and address the history of controversy.

The 2001 Roadless Rule prohibited road construction, road reconstruction, and timber harvesting on inventoried roadless areas (IRAs) on 58.5 million acres, or 31 percent, of National Forest System lands (defined in 36 CFR 294.11 (2001) identified in the set of maps contained in the 2001 Final EIS). The Rule provided for certain limited exceptions, including for forest stewardship purposes, public health and safety, reserved or outstanding rights, existing or renewed mineral leases, and realignment to prevent resource damage or address safety hazards (36 CFR 294.12 and 294.13 (2001)).

In the promulgation of the 2001 Roadless Rule, the Forest Service found that the Rule’s prohibitions on road construction and timber harvest were not likely to adversely affect threatened or endangered species or adversely modify designated or critical habitat; were not likely to jeopardize proposed species or adversely modify proposed critical habitat; and may beneficially affect threatened, endangered, and proposed species and critical habitat (USDA 2000). The Services concurred with the determination (66 FR 9 3271).

## Inventoried Roadless Areas

Today, inventoried roadless areas total approximately 58.2 million<sup>1</sup> acres, or about 30 percent of all National Forest System lands. The 2001 Roadless Rule applies to approximately 44.7 million acres of National Forest System lands, including about 9.3 million acres within the Tongass National Forest. The 44.7 million acres exclude approximately 9.3 million acres in Idaho and 4.2 million acres in Colorado, which are under state specific roadless rules. Current areas are reflected on national maps at the Forest Service Roadless Area website (https://www.fs.usda.gov/managing-land/planning/roadless)<sup>2</sup> .

More than 95 percent of IRAs managed under the 2001 Roadless Rule (outside of Colorado and Idaho) are in ten Western states (from largest inventoried roadless area acreage to smallest): Alaska, Montana, California, Utah, Wyoming, Nevada, Washington, Oregon, New Mexico, and Arizona. In the West, IRAs were typically 5,000 acres or greater. Some roadless areas are less than 5,000 acres—either because they are adjacent to Congressionally designated wilderness or in the eastern US (east of the 100th meridian). While they are referred to as “roadless”, because of the history of their inventory and designation, there are thousands of miles of existing roads within lands designated under the 2001 Roadless Rule, including National Forest System roads as well as roads of other ownerships. Additionally decommissioned roads and unauthorized routes that are not part of the official system or records may exist.

# PURPOSE AND NEED

The U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA or Department) is proposing to rescind the 2001 Roadless Area Conservation Rule (2001 Roadless Rule), which established broad prohibitions on road construction, road reconstruction, and timber harvesting within inventoried roadless areas on National Forest System lands. The intent of this proposed action is to reduce regulatory burden and return decision-making for the management of inventoried roadless areas to the land management planning process at the individual national forest level. Rescission of the nationallevel prohibitions provides responsible officials with flexibility to better guide management of National Forest System lands and respond to changing local resource conditions. # DESCRIPTION OF THE ACTION

The proposed action described below is a “framework programmatic action” as defined in 50 CFR 402.02, where the framework programmatic action only establishes a framework for the development of specific future action(s) but does not authorize any future action(s). The effects to listed species and designated critical habitat of future actions that are subsequently authorized, funded, or carried out would be addressed in subsequent section 7 consultations. While this is a ‘framework programmatic action’, it does not provide a new framework. Instead, the proposed rule would remove the Roadless Area Conservation Rule regulation/prohibitions from the broader existing legal framework in which the Forest Service plans, authorizes, and implements activities.  The programmatic effects are evaluated based on the legal framework that would remain in place, focusing on the effects of the removal of the prohibitions on roads construction and timber harvest (see Legal Framework, below).

Under the proposed rulemaking, the 2001 Roadless Area Conservation Rule prohibiting activities in inventoried roadless areas would be rescinded (also referred to as ‘repealed’). This action would eliminate the national prohibitions set forth in the 2001 Roadless Rule – road construction, reconstruction, and timber harvest may be authorized within inventoried roadless areas, where they are consistent with land management plans, or other statutory and regulatory requirements.  In addition, the proposed rule would remove the national “inventoried roadless area” designation from the 44.7 acres million acres of National Forest System lands currently designated as inventoried roadless areas under the 2001 Roadless Rule<sup>3</sup> .

Management of lands formerly managed as inventoried roadless areas would be governed by applicable land management plan direction alongside other relevant law, regulation, and policy. Some land management plans include components that restrict timber harvest or road building to meet other management objectives or legal requirements, such as to protect sensitive areas, to provide for primitive recreation opportunities, or to provide wildlife habitat. The proposed rescission of the Roadless Rule would not alter this existing land management plan direction.

Rescinding the Roadless Rule does not authorize, fund, or carry out future forest management activities that could occur in these areas.  All future site-specific project decisions, including those to authorize road construction, reconstruction, and timber harvesting, must be consistent with land management plans. Any proposed actions must undergo environmental review, including National Environmental Policy Act compliance prior to a decision. Project decisions must comply with applicable Federal and State laws, such as the Clean Water Act, the Clean Air Act, Endangered Species Act, National Historic Preservation Act, and the Wilderness Act, as well as provisions for government-to-government consultation with Tribes and Alaska Native Corporations.

In addition, the proposed rule would not compel the amendment or revision of any land management plan. While the rulemaking would not require such changes, plans may be amended in any manner whatsoever and are revised periodically (16 U.S.C. 1604(f)). Local responsible officials would continue to have discretion to initiate land management plan amendment or revise, as deemed necessary. A determination to amend or revise a land management plan is based on a variety of factors. Any future administrative changes, amendments, or revisions to land management plans would comply with NFMA and planning regulations (36 CFR 219 and the planning directives at FSM 1920 and FSH 1909.12), which includes substantive requirements to provide for species biodiversity, recovery of threatened and endangered species, and ecological conditions to provide viable populations of species of conservation concern. To the degree that a land management plan could change in the future, the effects of such a change would be attributed to and analyzed as part of the decision-making for that action.

Rescinding the Roadless Rule does not constrain or alter the statutory requirement of sections 7(a)(1) through section 7(a)(4) of the Endangered Species Act.  All future activities in currently inventoried roadless areas will require compliance with the ESA prior to implementation.

## Action Area Determination

There are 44.7 million acres of IRAs subject to the 2001 Roadless Rule, although only ~40 million acres are included in the action area where the proposed rulemaking could result in changes to future management activities. In this assessment, the term “action area” and “affected environment” are used interchangeably.

The action area for the proposed Roadless Rule Rescission was defined by removing nonNational Forest System lands, wilderness, wilderness study areas, and wild portions of wild and scenic rivers from Inventoried Roadless Areas managed under the 2001 Roadless Rule (resulting in ~40 million acres of action area) as potential future management of these areas would not be affected by the proposed rulemaking.

More specifically, review of the geospatial data found that 44.3 million acres of these are on National Forest System lands, meaning about 0.4 million acres of lands have had land ownership change since the 2001 Roadless Rule or there may be vertical integration problems (mapping errors) between the inventoried roadless data and official land ownership boundary data<sup>4</sup> .

In addition, over 4 million acres are within Congressionally designated wilderness<sup>5</sup> , wilderness study areas<sup>6</sup> , and wild segments of wild and scenic rivers<sup>7</sup> .  These statutory designations have more restrictive and more permanent mandates than the 2001 Roadless Rule. Therefore, changes in management activities in these areas under any alternative are highly unlikely and not reasonably certain to occur. The Forest Service did not arbitrarily buffer the action area to account for the possibility that some stressors such as noise and dust may extend beyond the IRA boundary. At the national scale, it is not reasonably certain that most stressors associated with a single action may extend beyond the boundary of the IRAs, except for sedimentation being carried downstream. ## Legal Framework for Future Projects<sup>8</sup>

As stated under the description of the action, the proposed rulemaking does not authorize any site-specific actions, however, it does remove prohibitions on certain actions such that they are reasonably certain to be proposed in some of these areas in the future (see also “Scope” below). Under the proposed rulemaking, all future projects that authorize road construction and timber harvest within the action area must still be consistent with all federal laws, including, Endangered Species Act (ESA), National Forest Management Act (NFMA) of 1976, National Environmental Policy Act, and, Clean Water Act, and must comply with the relevant land management plan. To ensure compliance with these laws and plans, during project-level environmental review and decision-making, the Forest Service includes project design measures that minimize<sup>9</sup> adverse effects to species and their habitats.

### National Forest Management Act

NFMA requires “guidelines for land management plans developed to achieve the goals of the Program which…provide for diversity of plant and animal communities based on the suitability and capability of the specific land area in order to meet overall multiple-use objectives, and within the multiple-use objectives of a land management plan adopted pursuant to this section, provide, where appropriate, to the degree practicable, for steps to be taken to preserve the diversity of tree species similar to that existing in the region controlled by the plan.”  NFMA is implemented through either the 2012 planning rule or the 1982 planning rule.

A primary purpose of the 2012 Planning Rule (planning rule) is to promote the ecological integrity of national forests and grasslands and other National Forest System administrative units. Ecological integrity – a substantive requirement of the planning rule – is designed to support Ecological Sustainability (36 CFR 219.8), Diversity of Plant and Animal Communities (36 CFR 219.9), and Multiple Uses (36 CFR 219.10). Similarly, the 1982 planning rule dictates “Fish and wildlife habitat shall be managed to maintain viable populations of existing native and desired non-native vertebrate species in the planning area. For planning purposes, a viable population shall be regarded as one which has the estimated numbers and distribution of reproductive individuals to insure its continued existence is well distributed in the planning area. In order to insure that viable populations will be maintained, habitat must be provided to support, at least, a minimum number of reproductive individuals and that habitat must be well distributed so that those individuals can interact with others in the planning area” and that “Forest planning shall provide for diversity of plant and animal communities and tree species consistent with the overall multiple-use objectives of the planning area. Such diversity shall be considered throughout the planning process. Inventories shall include quantitative data making possible the evaluation of diversity in terms of its prior and present condition. For each planning alternative, the interdisciplinary team shall consider how diversity will be affected by various mixes of resource outputs and uses, including proposed management practices. (Refer to Sec. 219.27(g).)”

All forest and grassland management plans, regardless of which planning rule they follow, must be consulted on under the Endangered Species Act.

#### _Aquatic Species Conservation Strategies_

Specific to aquatic species habitat, NFMA mandates that U.S. Forest Service protect streams, streambanks, shorelines, lakes, and wetlands from detrimental changes in water temperature, sediment deposits, and blocked watercourses during timber harvesting and management. It requires, through land management plans, that soil and water resources are not irreversibly damaged, often by establishing riparian management areas and utilizing Best Management Practices (BMPs). Further, individual land management plans contain standards and guides for aquatic management zones, some in the form of Aquatic Conservation Strategies (ACS) (such as the Northwest Forest Plan (USFS and USBLM 1994b), PacFish (USFS 1995a), and InFish (USFS 1995b)). Aquatic conservation strategies and land management plans standards and guides are designed to move the aquatic management zones to desired ecological conditions.

Aquatic conservation strategies use an ecosystem approach to management of riparian and aquatic habitats and were designed to (1) protect watersheds that had good-quality habitat and strong fish populations at the time they were drafted, and (2) halt further declines in watershed condition and restore ecological processes that create and maintain favorable conditions in aquatic ecosystems in degraded ecosystems (FEMAT 1993). The long-term goal (100+ years) is to develop a network of functioning watersheds that supports populations of fish and other aquatic and riparian-dependent organisms (such as the Northwest Forest Plan (USFS and USBLM 1994b), PacFish (USFS 1995a), and InFish (USFS 1995b)). Aquatic conservation strategies are based on preserving and restoring key ecological processes, including the natural disturbance regimes (USFS and USBLM 1994b) that create and maintain habitat for native aquatic and riparian-dependent organisms, and it recognizes that periodic natural disturbances may be required to sustain ecological productivity. As a result, aquatic conservation strategies do not expect all watersheds to be in favorable condition (highly productive for the same aquatic organisms) at any point in time, nor does it expect that any watershed will remain in a certain condition through time. The primary objective of aquatic conservation strategies is to maintain and restore the distribution, diversity, and complexity of watershed-level features and processes to which aquatic and riparian species are uniquely adapted. Aquatic conservation strategies were also designed to develop a strategy to guide management of aquatic ecosystems on federal lands, where amended, that would meet potential ESA requirements (Reeves et al. 2018). Aquatic conservation strategies were expected to make significant contributions to the recovery of the ESA-listed fish by increasing the quantity and quality of freshwater habitat for Pacific salmon and protecting and enhancing habitats of other species (Reeves et al. 2006, FEMAT 1993).

### Best Management Practices (Clean Water Act)

Forest Service National Best Management Practices for Water Quality Management on National Forest System Lands (USFS 2012a) was developed to improve agency performance and accountability in managing water quality consistent with the Federal Clean Water Act (CWA) and State water quality programs. Current Forest Service policy directs compliance with required CWA permits and State regulations and requires the use of BMPs to control nonpoint source pollution to meet applicable water quality standards and other CWA requirements. The National BMP program document provides consistency among Forest Service administrative units to efficiently administer the program and demonstrate improvements in performance and accountability at multiple scales. The National BMP Program consists of four main components: (1) a set of National Core BMPs, (2) a set of standardized monitoring protocols to evaluate implementation and effectiveness of those BMPs, (3) a data management and reporting structure, and (4) corresponding national direction.

### Relationship Between the 2001 Roadless Rule and Land Management Planning

Administration of each national forest and grassland is directed by a land management plan, along with numerous land management laws, regulations, policies, and agency directives. A land management plan provides a framework for future site-specific project analysis and decision making.  Land management plans are developed, amended, and revised under the National Forest Management Act and the 2012 Planning Rule at 36 CFR 219. All land management plans must conform to existing laws and regulations. Laws and regulations take precedence over management direction in the land management plans. When new or changed laws or regulations create conflicts with land management plan content, plans are administratively changed as needed to conform to new statutory and regulatory requirements (36 CFR 219.1(f) and 219.13(c)).

The 2001 Roadless Rule did not amend, revise, or compel amendment or revision of any land management plans (36 CFR 294.14(b) (2001); 66 FR 3273); however, the Roadless Rule supersedes individual land management plans where the provision of the rule is more restrictive than a plan. Similarly, for some areas, the plans may contain plan components that are more restrictive than the 2001 Roadless Rule. Under the 2012 Planning Rule, inventoried roadless areas are treated as a type of designated area,<sup>10</sup> with the option to also identify these areas as unique geographic areas or management areas in the plans. Plans may also include additional plan components for inventoried roadless areas if they are compatible with the restrictions of the applicable roadless rule. Within land management plans, inventoried roadless areas currently overlap other management areas or geographic areas, such as core habitats, research natural areas, alpine areas, or areas managed for backcountry recreation where there are expected to be low levels of development, including timber harvest and road construction.

Current land management-plans often include plan direction and components that identify where timber management and road construction may or may not be allowed. Example management areas where timber management or road construction may be limited include: recommended wilderness, research natural areas, or backcountry management areas. ## Scope

The following describes how the agency drew a reasonable and manageable line in consideration of effects of the proposed action that are reasonably certain to occur, and those that may extend outside the geographical territory of the proposal or might materialize later in time.

In general, it is reasonably likely activities with potential for beneficial or adverse environmental effects could occur within or near inventoried roadless areas following the proposed rulemaking. However, the rulemaking would not compel, require, or authorize any site-specific activities. Therefore, site-specific locations and amounts of activities that could occur in the future under the alternatives are uncertain. Any future decisions on these activities are greatly influenced by factors beyond the proposed rulemaking such as local decision-making through land management plans and projects, local industry capacity, agency budget allocations and resources, and other regulations and policies. Site-specific effects can be meaningfully measured and evaluated at the project and activity stage and are evaluated during the project-level ESA Section 7 consultation process. In addition, some of the potential adverse effects described could be reduced or mitigated at the project level, through complying with policy, following land management plan direction, implementing best management practices, or developing projectspecific design criteria.  Protections/requirements that reduce impacts are noted throughout this analysis.

The analysis focuses on the difference where (what locations) activities, particularly road construction and timber harvest, are reasonably likely to occur within inventoried roadless areas, and the resulting effects to listed species. Estimates of areas where activities could occur, were prepared for effects analysis purposes – they do not reflect proposed or planned activity areas. The stressors associated with these activities were assumed to potentially occur when species would be present and affected since the timing of future activities is not known.

The geographic extent of the environmental effects analysis focuses on activities with a close causal relationship to the rulemaking. Changes to regulations remain in place until additional rulemaking or Congressional action. Short- and long-term reasonably likely to occur environmental impacts of the rulemaking are considered.

The short-term potential for road construction and timber harvest or related activities and associated effects is more predictable and reasonably likely to occur. Within the potentially affected environment the following areas are more likely to have activities or impacts, in the short term (5 years):

- areas where activities are allowed under existing land management plans (see environmental baseline below);

- areas with a greater management priority or ‘need’ (ex. high risk firesheds and areas affected by insect and disease or other disturbances);

- areas that are more feasible or economical for equipment and workers to access (more operable); and,

- areas with high product potential/economic viability for timber production to support management objectives.

In the longer term (up to 20 years and beyond), the extent of activities is more uncertain. Still, it is reasonable to expect a greater proportion of the potentially effected environment could be impacted by activities over time. Factors affecting long term change include natural events such as storms and wildfires, local decision-making through projects and land management plan amendments/revisions, national policy changes, technological changes, industry and economic changes, etc. Areas that are least likely for development in the short term (currently restricted, less developed, etc.) could be impacted by change, in the longer term. However, given the long causal chain and distance from the action proposed, predicting the specific amounts is too speculative.

The Roadless Rule prohibited road construction and timber harvest (with exemptions) but did not directly restrict other activities.  Rescinding the Roadless Rule increases the likelihood of timber and road construction activities occurring within the boundary of previously designated IRAs. However, removing the prohibitions on road construction in IRAs may increase accessibility and therefore, increase use of the land for purposes other than timber harvest. These activities are speculative, not reasonably certain to occur, and would require a lengthy causal chain to tie to the proposed rescission of the Roadless Rule. As such, only timber and road construction activities are considered in this analysis.

### Road Construction

**Road construction**<sup>**11**</sup> **or road reconstruction**<sup>**12**</sup> **or road maintenance**<sup>**13**</sup> may be associated with timber harvest or other forest vegetation management (fuels reduction, thinning, etc.), private or special uses, existing rights, minerals and energy exploration<sup>14</sup> , watershed restoration, habitat improvement, and recreation development. Aside from timber harvest, these activities are currently allowed under the 2001 Roadless Rule (see environmental baseline section).

The objective of the NFS transportation system is to provide the minimal mileage road system needed for safe and efficient travel, and for administration, utilization and protection of NFS lands. Any new roads would be designed and maintained in a way that would minimize negative effects on water quality, soil stability, and wildlife habitat. The decision to construct a new, permanent road that would become part of the NFS road system would be made as part of transportation planning associated with planned resource management activities and use of NFS lands. Proposals for new roads related to development and the associated environmental effects would be considered through project-level planning. If future decisions authorize it, modifications to the transportation network would follow law, regulation and policy found at 16 USC 1608, FSM/FSH 7700, relevant State and Federal statutes such as the Clean Water Act, NEPA, and Endangered Species Act, and national forest land management plans. Implementation of these policies can minimize, but not eliminate, some adverse environmental effects.

### Timber Harvest

**Vegetation and Forest Management** include commercial timber harvest<sup>17</sup> and non-commercial timber treatments<sup>18</sup> and are completed for multiple resource objectives, including but not limited to timber production, fuels reduction, public health and safety, forest health improvement, watershed improvement, and wildlife habitat improvement. Land management plans must ensure: (1) Timber harvest is only allowed in areas that will not be irreversibly damaged, where cutting units can be visually blended into the terrain, where streams, wetlands, riparian areas, and water bodies are protected; (2) Timber harvest does not exceed sustained yield and is not designated solely to maximize outputs; and, (3) Harvested areas must be regenerated within 5 years. Any project activity, including timber harvest, must be consistent with land management plans. The 2012 Planning Rule provides further direction for forest management activities including timber harvest and non-commercial treatments. These plans include plan components (desired conditions, goals, objectives, standards, and guidelines) and identify areas suitable and not suitable for timber production to maintain sustained yield levels, identify maximum harvest levels on suitable lands (allowable sale quantity or sustained yield limit), and allow, prohibit, or limit timber harvest to protect resources.

## Data Collection and Use

We acquired species occurrence data from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) and Natural Resource Manager (NRM) using R and ArcGIS Pro, respectively. The databases do not differentiate between DPSes of vertebrate species listed under the ESA, which causes potential inflation of exposure to these entities. The data is managed in such a way that occasionally species occurrences occur in locations where the entity is not listed or protected by the ESA; however, the FS does not believe these discrepancies have altered the conclusions provided.

A custom exposure analysis is performed for some species known to be impacted by roads and timber activities and where national data could support such analyses.  Where custom analyses are performed, they will be documented in either the administrative record or in the taxonomic sections below.  While the potential for inflated exposure is possible the FS does not believe it would significantly affect the determinations made. These data discrepancies will also be addressed during project-level consultation. We processed GBIF data using three Python scripts to unzip the downloaded GBIF folders and join relevant data (e.g., NLCD, basic ownership, Roadless Rule Affected Environments), filter to ESA sensitive species, and summarize the data into region-wide csv files. For Forest Service Alaska Region (Region 10), we separated the GBIF data from the conterminous United States (CONUS) data and ran a separate Python script due to available NLCD data (2024 Annual NLCD data for CONUS and the 2016 NLCD Alaska data for Region 10). We designated NRM data features using ArcGIS Pro’s ArcPy as either inside or outside of Roadless Rule Affected Environments in National Forest land. We then determined each feature’s NLCD value and class using pixel count majorities in R, joined relevant data (e.g., basic ownership and Roadless Rule Affected Environments), created Forest Service region-wide summaries, and exported them as an excel workbook.  Species occurrence data are opportunistic and do not represent the results of exhaustive structured species surveys nor do they include negative occurrence data. Additionally, for mobile species, an observation of an individual in a specific location does not mean that that location is occupied by resident individuals- some of these observations could have been of transient individuals.

Exposure was determined by comparing species occurrence data with roadless area operability (as defined in the Environmental Baseline section below) and categorized as high, moderate, and low likelihood of exposure.  Low exposure was determined for species that had no occurrences in IRAs. Medium exposure was determined for species with less than 50% of occurrences in IRAs. High exposure was determined for species that had greater than 50% of occurrences in IRAs. Where there was no occurrence data the exposure was classified as unknown. Exposure estimates were performed in a manner to err on the side of likely exposure. Existing protections afforded by land management plans and other documents/decisions were also reviewed to determine how they may affect the determinations for each species, particularly if effects to the species were determined to be highly impactful.  The response of species was derived from published scientific literature and previous consultations.  Together, exposure and response were used to make the determinations: No effect (NE), Not likely to adversely affect (NLAA), and likely to adversely affect (LAA).

## Land Management Plans and Operability

A national review of plan components in management areas overlapping the potentially affected environment was completed to identify where plans do or do not further restrict timber harvest and road construction activities<sup>20</sup> . There is much variation between individual land management plans and Regions reflecting differences in local conditions, age of the plans, and changes in planning policies over time. Current land management-plans include plan direction and components that identify where timber management and road construction uses may or may not be allowed (Table 3). Example management area types where timber management or road construction may be limited include: recommended wilderness, research natural areas, or backcountry management areas are administrative land management planning designations that limit active management.

- Across the Roadless Affected Environment, land management plan components generally allow timber harvest across 13.4 million acres (33.4% of NFS land within the Roadless Affected Environment).  [36% not allowed, 26.8 generally not allowed (with some exceptions)].

- Across the Roadless Affected Environment, land management plan components generally allow road construction across 18.2 million acres (45.5% of NFS land within the Roadless Affected Environment).

This analysis is directed at actions that are reasonably likely to occur in the remaining acres of inventoried roadless areas where road construction and reconstruction or timber harvest would be most likely to occur under current land management plans. A nationwide geospatial analysis was used to estimate potential operability, or the capability of mechanized treatment or activities, in each area based on slope, distance from roads, site productivity, and statutory restrictions (Table 2):

- Likely Operable: Less than 0.5 miles from a road, on slopes less than 40%, are productive for restocking (greater than 20 cubic feet/acre/year), are potentially forested, and have no statutory restrictions from timber harvest.

- Likely Operable but Complex: Less than 0.5 miles from a road and on slopes between 40% and 70% or between 0.5 miles and one mile from a road and on slopes less than 70%. These areas have the same productivity for restocking as likely operable areas, are potentially forested, and no statutory restrictions from timber harvest.

- Likely Not Operable: Include slopes over 70%, further than one mile from a road or areas over one mile from a road and less than 70% percent slope. These areas have low productivity, are potentially forested, and no statutory restrictions from timber harvest.

## General Characteristics of the Affected Environment

Roadless Rule prohibitions do not mean no activities occur in Inventoried Roadless Areas. Activities that are ongoing and permissible, if not restricted by land management plans or other law, regulation, and policy include, but are not limited to:

- Access for reserved or outstanding rights/existing rights for minerals

- Continuation of existing land use authorizations (ex. utilities, recreation special uses, private access, easements, etc.)

- Road maintenance on existing roads

- Motorized and non-motorized trail construction or maintenance;

- Hunting, fishing, hiking, camping, and other recreational uses;

- Use of a motorized vehicle on a trail open to motorized use;

- Mountain biking on a trail open to mechanized use;

- Prescribed burning; and

- Livestock grazing.

The 2001 Roadless Rule prohibition on road construction and reconstruction in IRAs did not affect already existing access, occupancy, and use rights. These rights include those that exist by law, treaty, or by other authority. They include, but are not limited to, the right to provide reasonable access across NFS lands to private property, mining claims for locatable minerals under the 1872 Mining Law, and land uses protected by Native American treaty rights. These rights include, but are not limited to, rights of access provided in the ANILCA (Public Law 96487), and recognized highway rights-of-way granted over NFS lands under Revised Statute 2477 (Public Law 94-579). The most common type of access pursued in conjunction with these two prominent statues is motorized road access. Land use authorizations and rights-of-way existing prior to the 2001 Roadless Rule are ongoing and part of baseline conditions.

While they are referred to as “roadless”, because of the decades between inventory and designation, there are thousands of miles of existing roads within lands designated under the 2001 Roadless Rule, including National Forest System roads as well as roads of other ownerships. Additionally decommissioned roads and unauthorized routes that are not part of the official system or records may exist.

Between 1984 and 2024, wildfire burned 20.5% of potentially affected IRAs (or 29.9% of potentially affected IRAs outside of Alaska). Of these, 4.2% (1.7 million acres) were high severity wildfire and 5.2% (2.1 million acres) were moderate severity wildfire. Nationally, wildfire hazards are commonly characterized using the Wildfire Hazard Potential (WHP) index, which integrates burn probability and potential fire intensity. Across potentially affected IRAs outside of Alaska, 42 percent of lands are classified as high or very high Wildfire Hazard Potential, indicating a high potential for intense wildfire under severe weather conditions. Regional values range from 5 percent in the Eastern Region to 60 percent in the Pacific Southwest Region. Within potentially affected IRAs 9.8 million acres (24 percent) overlap with Healthy Forests Restoration Act Wildland Urban Interface. Within the potentially affected environment 5.9 million acres (14.8 percent) overlap with Community Wildfire Risk Reduction Zones (CWRRZ). Nearly all CWRRZ within the potentially affected environment occurs within the transition zone (within the 1.5‑mile buffer surrounding building clusters).

## 2001 Roadless Area Conservation Rule Implementation

The 2001 Roadless Rule provided that the Responsible Official may grant certain limited exceptions to the road construction and timber harvest prohibitions, including for forest stewardship purposes, public health and safety, reserved or outstanding rights, existing or renewed mineral leases, and realignment of roads to prevent resource damage or address safety hazards (36 CFR 294.12(b) and 294.13(b) (2001)). It also provides exceptions to the timber harvest prohibitions for generally small diameter tree cutting to improve habitat for threatened, endangered, or sensitive species or to maintain or restore ecosystem characteristics and where it maintains or enhances one or more of the roadless characteristics, or when the activity is incidental to activities not otherwise prohibited (36 CFR 294.13(b) (2001)). The rule states that use of the timber harvest exceptions would be expected to be infrequent. Amid legal challenges to the 2001 Roadless Rule and to provide for consistent application, exceptions for specific activities in IRAs were reviewed and authorized by the Secretary and Forest Service Chief until 2012. In 2012, certain exceptions were delegated to the applicable regional forester. In 2018 all exceptions were delegated to the regional foresters, and some exceptions for public health and safety or small diameter tree cutting<sup>21</sup> may be further delegated to responsible officials. Approval of exceptions generally requires review documentation, including maps and justification.

Regional forester approval of exceptions under the 2001 Roadless Rule has been limited, and the majority have been for stewardship purposes. There is no national-level tracking of the review, approval, or implementation of 2001 Roadless Rule exceptions, though the Washington Office has recommended that Responsible Officials document reviews and approvals. Regional level tracking of review and approvals between 2018 and 2025 shows about 188 documented projects were granted exceptions. About 10 percent involved road related activities and 96 percent involved tree cutting (some involved both road and tree cutting activities). Since 2018, the majority were related to maintaining and restoring ecosystem characteristics. Eight exceptions approved tree cutting for the explicit purpose of “improving threatened, endangered, or sensitive species habitat,” although some of the exceptions outside of this category may have served multiple purposes, such as providing benefits to species habitat. This information does not include responsible official reviews or approvals below the regional forester level<sup>22</sup> , so it is not possible to list all exceptions that were granted with existing data sources.

Land management plans typically contain aquatic management strategies that have standards and guidelines for activities that can and cannot occur in the aquatic management zones. There are three examples of aquatic management strategies within the action area: the Northwest Forest Plan (NWFP) (USDA and USDI 1994); the interim strategies for managing anadromous fishproducing watersheds in eastern Oregon and Washington, Idaho, and Portions of California (PacFish) (USDA and USDI 1995); and the Inland native fish strategy (InFish) (USDA 1995). Within these aquatic strategies, riparian management objectives considered in each strategy need to be met before allowing certain activities to occur within the aquatic management zones. Aquatic management zones are generally identified within the three strategies as 1 site-potentialtree height (spth) or 150 feet, whichever is greater. Land Management Plans outside of these areas may have similar protections for the aquatic management zones. Forests where these aquatic management strategies do not apply, may have their own aquatic conservation strategy or utilize their land management plan standards and guides when determining what actions are allowable within the aquatic management zone. Effects to Pacific Salmon and Steelhead and their Critical Habitat

Pacific salmon and steelhead are anadromous fish, meaning adults spend most of their life in the ocean but migrate into fresh water to spawn. The effects of Forest Service actions are almost always within the freshwater systems these salmonids utilize during their life cycles. Table 10 shows the effects pathway to species compared to the impacts/benefits described in the deconstruction table.

The proximity and probability of actions occurring near listed salmonid species or their designated critical habitat (sometimes referred to as listed fish habitat (LFH)) are assumed to occur at some point in the future. Specific ESUs and DPSs for the salmonids and which National Forest projects could cause effects to them are in Table 3. Maps of salmonid critical habitats are in Appendix A. Maps and shown in Figures 1 through 27. The magnitude of effects to species and critical habitat are assumed to be localized to the action area based on the aquatic conservation strategies listed above. Action distribution typically will not occur within an entire watershed during the same year but will be dependent on how timber sales are planned and the outcome of future NEPA and ESA consultation. This could mean several small effects in a watershed or one large effect for the watershed could occur. Frequency of actions would be based on many different factors, including the need for timber, but timber harvest in one area typically span decades since this timeframe is dependent on timber growth.

### Change in Riparian Vegetation (Temperature)

Change in riparian vegetation is associated with stream temperature. Tree canopies disrupt the solar radiation from reaching the stream and increasing stream temperatures. Timber felling, landing creation and yarding corridors typically involve the loss of the tree canopy resulting in shade loss.

While stream shade correlates with the width of stream protection buffers, the relationship is quite variable, depending on site-specific factors such as stream size, substrate type, stream discharge, topography (Caissie 2006), channel aspect, and forest structure and species composition. Inputs of cold water from the streambed, seepage areas on the stream bank, and tributaries can help cool the stream on hot summer days if they are sufficiently large relative to the stream discharge (Wondzell 2012). The density of vegetation in riparian areas affects shade and thermal loading to a stream due to the penetration of solar radiation through gaps in the canopy and among the branches and stems (Brazier and Brown 1973; DeWalle 2010). In some instances (such as narrow streams with dense, overhanging streamside vegetation, or stands on the north sides of streams with an east-west orientation), no-harvest buffers as narrow as 30 feet adjacent to clearcuts can maintain stream shade (Brazier and Brown 1973). Wider buffers, in general, will provide increased protection of stream temperature (Anderson et al. 2007, Leinenbach et al. 2013, Groom et al. 2011a, Groom et al. 2011b).

Timber harvest adjacent to riparian buffers can influence the riparian microclimate adjacent to streams, which can, in turn, influence stream temperatures (Moore et al. 2005a). Gomi et al. (2006) reported that inclusion of a 30m (98’) buffer in clear-cut harvesting units increased maximum daily temperatures by <2˚C in British Columbia. Wilkerson et al. (2006) reported maximum increases of 1.0-1.4 ˚C with 11 m buffers and negligible increases with 23m (75') or partially cut buffers in Maine. DeWalle (2010) theorized that at least 80% shade could be attained with 12m (39') wide buffers on small (<6m [20'] wide) streams. In western Oregon, Zwieniecki and Newton (1999) reported that the presence of near continuous shade from an uncut buffer positioned on the south side of water led to protection from the effect of solar radiation. On the Oregon Coast Range, Groom et al. (2011a) found that on post-harvest lands with a protection buffer (no-cut zone) of 6m (20’) and limited harvest in the remainder of the riparian management area increased stream temperature by an average of 0.7 degrees C, but no change to maximum stream temperatures on lands managed to the more stringent state standard of 8m (26’) protection buffer and limited harvest in the remainder of the riparian management zone. The high variability in buffer width and shade condition is a function of the many variables that influence the amount of shade produced by riparian vegetation. Low-density stands with limited vertical distribution of branches and foliage will require wider buffer widths to produce the same amount of shade as high-density stands.

Riparian treatments on perennial streams greater than 1,000’ from LFH are not expected to increase stream temperatures in LFH when there is hyporheic flow and flow accumulation. Several papers have been published recently indicating that hyporheic flow and stream substrate composition has a significant influence on stream temperature. Janishch et al. (2012) found that that the canopy cover of “buffers” was not a strong predictor for temperature in small (<7 feet wide) headwater streams. Streams with coarse-textured streambeds tended to be thermally unresponsive as compared with fine-textured streambeds or those with small, near-stream wetland areas. Moore et al. (2005b) found that heat exchange associated with hyporheic flow paths was a cooling effect during daytime, with a magnitude up to approximately 25% that of net radiation during the period of maximum daytime warming in clear cut harvest units on streams in British Columbia. Davis et al. (2015) found that in Western Oregon streams, pre-harvest to postharvest temperature approximately 300m downstream of the harvest will be 56% of the temperature change that occurred within the harvest reach. Rapid cooling has been observed after an extensive exposure to the sun after water re-entered forest cover in Australia (Rutherford et al. 2004), and, in Idaho, warming of headwater streams with no buffers did not lead to warming of fish-bearing streams downstream (Gravelle and Link 2007). Zwieniecki and Newton (1999) indicated that stream temperature increases from harvesting activity were frequently lost within 150–300 m (492-984') beneath downstream cover. This re-emphasizes the important role gravel and large wood play in channels. Therefore, thinning on perennial channels 1,000’ or greater from LFH is expected to have only minor negative effects to temperature in LFH.

Accurately predicting the magnitude of stream temperature increases on a highly variable landscape is extremely difficult. However, the research cited above supports the conclusion that while there may be a decrease in effective shade, localized increases in stream temperature are likely to be too small to differentiate from background variability. If localized increases in stream temperature occur, they will likely be less than 1 degree C and are unlikely to be measurable in LFH. The tributary streams that flow through the harvest units are small in size (<1-5 cfs in most cases) and research has shown that the magnitude of temperature effect is a function of the amount of water entering (or exchanged with) the stream relative to the stream discharge (Johnson and Jones 2000, Story et al. 2003, Wondzell 2006, Wondzell 2012) and the difference in temperature between the stream and the inflowing water. Therefore, any localized increase in stream temperature from commercial thinning activities would typically be minor negative effect on LFH streams.

Typically, timber harvest activities are associated with an aquatic conservation strategy or land management plan component that provides for a stream protection buffer to limit the solar radiation reaching the stream. It is probable future projects will include some type of minimization measure for stream buffers for LFH areas, but since specific buffers are unknown for now, this analysis assumes there could be projects that will have limited stream protection buffers and therefore have effects on stream temperature. As described above, most of the time timber harvest activities result in insignificant localized stream temperature increases because rapid cooling should occur as the stream flows into the uncut aquatic management zone buffer; therefore, effects should last less than an hour.

Road maintenance typically includes tree removal or brushing near streams and has the potential to create small minor changes to stream temperature. If there are drainage needs for road maintenance, such as culvert replacements, there may be a need to dewater the area and move it to a different location using pumps and screens. Depending on how this is accomplished, it could cause water temperatures to increase. If there are increases in the water temperature, the changes should be minor but could be measurable as future projects are unknown at this time. Past consultations have provided minimization measures that limit effects to temperature fluctuations.

While the specific future road maintenance and reconstruction, permanent and temporary road construction actions are uncertain, if those future actions are implemented within one site potential tree height there would be some shade loss from timber harvest, . Effects are expected to mostly be insignificant, unmeasurable effects, but could result in small localized measurable increases in stream temperatures. Temporary road removal would not create measurable effects to increases in stream water temperature and can regrow shrubs quickly in the area, which would be beneficial.

Overall, under the proposed rulemaking, where future road construction and timber harvest activities occur within the action area, they would have small measurable effects on salmonids and their critical habitat.

### Chemical Contamination/Nutrients

Chemical contamination and nutrients for projects in this consultation are associated with equipment utilizing petroleum products and dust abatement chemicals. Timber vegetation management, road maintenance, road construction, and road removal activities all utilize heavy machinery, some utilize hand tools (for example, chainsaws), water pumps, and dust abatement chemicals have the potential to create chemical contamination reaching the streams.

Coarse woody debris is an important component of forest ecosystems because it can provide cover for many wildlife species, provide important structures for small mammals, reptiles, and amphibians, and the decomposition of logs on the forest floor that provides nutrients to plants, habitat for wildlife, and food for insects and microorganisms. This process of nutrient input is important for the food web processes for salmonids as they feed on insects and microorganisms during certain life stages. Where timber harvest has been implemented, replanting is important for this process to continue. Harvest areas are typically localized to units and timber harvest must meet certain criteria if it is to be implemented within the aquatic management zones. For many of the forests in the action area, future projects will maintain a no-cut buffer for the aquatic management zone. This will insulate some food-web negative effects.

Any work utilizing chemicals and petroleum products typically requires an approved Spill Prevention Control and Containment Plan (Spill Plan) as required by contract provisions G.3.4.1/BT6.341. The Spill Plan would include a description of the hazardous materials that would be used; and a spill containment kit would be located on-site. All heavy equipment used for refueling would carry a hazardous material recovery kit. All vehicles and machinery would be free of petroleum leaks. Any leaks that occur would be immediately repaired. Power equipment would be refueled at least 150 feet from water bodies or as far away as possible to prevent direct delivery of contaminants into a water body. If local site conditions do not allow for a 150-foot setback, then refueling would be as far away as possible from the water body (USDA 2012). For all immobile equipment, absorbent pads would be used. All petroleum products being transported or stored would be in approved containers meeting Occupational Safety and Health Administration and appropriate state standards. The Forest Service Contracting Officer would be notified of any spills. Any contaminated soil, vegetation or debris must be removed from National Forest System lands and disposed of in accordance with state laws.

Fuel spills of petroleum products from heavy equipment for all management activities have the potential to result in chemical contamination of streams. Hand tool fuel capacity is small (<1/4 gallon), and thus any potential spills during refueling would be unlikely to reach the stream channel resulting in a neutral effect for hand tools. Water pump fuel capacity is also small, and refueling is completed outside of the stream. It is extremely unlikely that heavy equipment in these activities will spill contaminants due to the contract clause requiring the Spill Plan. It is a standard contract clause that vehicles are checked regularly for leaks by purchasers and timber sale administrators and any leaks are promptly repaired before the vehicle is allowed to continue operating. Based on over 2 decades of experience that encompasses tens of thousands of log truck loads, as well as contractor supplied equipment for instream and road work, there is very low probability of chemical spills, and there have been no known spills from these activities that have impacted LFH. Forest Service best management practices require spill kits to be available if there is a spill and require fueling stations to be located 150 feet from streams when possible, and as far as practical when not possible.

The Forest Service expects limited applications for dust abatement. Dust abatement is typically only implemented during the dry season. Dust abatement can utilize water and chemicals (for example, lignosulfonate, Magnesium Chloride), depending on the conditions in the work area. The Forest Service has a guide on applications for dust suppression (USDA 1999). The application of dust abatement chemicals would reduce fine sediment delivery to streams typically from dry season haul. Several different chemicals are discussed in the Forest Service guide, with pros and cons of each including an environmental impact section. Most chemicals do have negative environmental impacts, and these will be taken into consideration when utilized.

Lignosulfonate is an organic, nonpetroleum product that has limited ability to migrate since it is a bonding agent that adheres to material in dry conditions. Lignosulfonate is the chemical expected to be utilized near listed fish habitat, but there may be other chemicals utilized as well. There is potential for the dust abatement product to directly enter waterways. However, the single application of lignosulfonate during the dry season would reduce the likelihood of it entering a stream channel. There would not be an effect to LFH fish from applying lignosulfonate on road surfaces. Lignosulfonate has been tested to determine its potential impact on water quality and fish species when introduced directly into surface waters and it is rather benign. A 48-hour LC50 of 7,300 parts per million (ppm) was observed by Roald (1977) in rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss). Compounds with values greater than a value of >1,000 are considered to have a low order of toxicity. Due to the limited use and application of lignosulfonate, the timing of application, the use of best management practices for application, and the known non-toxicity to fish at dust abatement concentrations, the probability of affecting listed fish is discountable.

Magnesium chloride can negatively impact aquatic systems by reducing macroinvertebrates (Kotalik et al. 2017) and directly affecting fish (Lewis 1998). If Magnesium chloride is utilized, there will be direct and indirect effects to fish through death (at high enough concentrations) and food web processes. The magnitude of effect is difficult to determine without having project specific information. Future project consultation will determine which chemical is utilized for dust abatement if it is needed but if utilized, we hypothesize the effects will be short-term but not discountable.

Timber harvest, road maintenance and reconstruction, and temporary road removal could cause short-term negative direct effects to the transport of nutrients downstream when implementing the projects, however, when projects utilize a water bypass system, effects would be limited to project implementation. These activities could provide for long-term beneficial effects when maintaining or repairing the current drainage system, installing appropriate sized drainage systems for the stream, and utilizing water bypass systems during the maintenance process.

Road construction could cause short-term negative direct effects to the transport of nutrients downstream when implementing the projects and fuel spills of petroleum products from machinery and equipment used in road work have the potential to result in chemical contamination of streams. Fuel powered equipment (hand tools and heavy machinery) will be used within the Riparian Reserves for road construction, including over stream crossings, where they are needed.

Overall, under the proposed rulemaking, where future road construction and timber harvest activities occur within the action area, they would have small measurable effects on salmonids and their critical habitat.

### Drainage Network

The natural drainage networks of streams and rivers can be increased through road building and impervious road ditching and channeling that redirects surface flow and increase the active channel network. The primary management effect is associated with the road network, especially road compaction and road drainage systems. An increased drainage network can increase peak volume discharge and cause peak flows to occur earlier than unroaded drainages.

Wemple et al. (1996) studied the effect of channel network extension on two basins in the western Cascades of Oregon and found that 57% of the road network was connected to the stream network (road segments draining to streams, or road segments draining to culvert outlets with gullies incised below them), resulting in an increased drainage density of 21-50%. They concluded that this increase in road drainage density, and enhanced water routing efficiency was a possible explanatory mechanism for the changes in hydrograph shape (such as earlier peak, higher volume discharge) as presented in Jones and Grant (1996). Many other watershed factors (for example, soil percolation rates, soil stability, road construction type) may influence the magnitude or probability that road extension will affect flows.

System road reconstruction, road (system and non-system) maintenance, and permanent (system roads) and temporary (non-system roads) roads will be built, and decommissioning may occur in areas adjacent to streams with listed fish habitat (LFH) as well as in areas that may be several miles upstream from LFH.

Road reconstruction and maintenance have the potential for reducing the distance water travels along roadways, with the implementation of larger cross drain culverts, reduced spacing between culverts, and/or better placement of near-stream cross drains and their outflows. Road Decommissioning may occur near LFH streams, or on tributary streams that feed into LFH streams.

There are no causal effects of timber harvest on the drainage network, and the effects of timber harvest would be neutral to this element.

The only causal mechanism for effects for road maintenance and road re-construction would be through snow plowing. These would create short-term negative effects during the spring when snow melts. These would be localized effects to the area where snow plowing occurs. Snow plowing is not an action that is routinely implemented on National Forest System roads, so the probability of occurrence is low, but not zero. If effects do occur to drainage networks, these effects would be minor and localized.

Permanent and temporary road construction will have negative effects for drainage network lasting as long as the road is in place.

Temporary road removal will have long-term beneficial effects on drainage network. The process would be the opposite of road building.

Overall, activities will have small measurable effects on salmonids and their critical habitat.

Large Woody Debris

Large woody debris enters streams through chronic and episodic processes (Bisson et al. 1987). Chronic sources include streamside tree mortality and bank undercutting (Murphy and Koski 1989). Episodic sources include windthrow (for example, Harmon et al. 1986; Lienkaemper and Swanson 1986; May and Gresswell 2003), wildfire (Agee 1993), infrequent flooding, landslides/debris flows (Benda et al. 2002, 2003), and insect infestations or disease. The quality, complexity, and function of fish habitat is often affected by the level of instream large woody debris. Large woody debris influences the size and distribution of stream habitat units, provides cover from predators, shades the water surface, and increases the sorting of substrate, providing suitable spawning areas (Megahan 1982; Bilby and Ward 1991; Crispin et al. 1993; Montgomery et al.1995; May and Gresswell 2003). Large woody debris is instrumental in nutrient retention and provides a substrate for macroinvertebrates (Cederholm and Peterson 1985; Bilby and Ward 1989; Bilby and Bisson 1998; Gregory et al. 1991; Wallace et al. 1995). Large woody debris influences channel morphology (Swanson and Lienkaemper 1978; Heede 1985; Bisson et al. 1987; Swanston 1991). Fish densities are higher in streams with abundant large woody debris (Fausch and Northcote 1992; Roni and Quinn 2001b).

Peak/Baseflows

Peak/baseflows can be defined as quantity of water and the timing of input of water to an area. Loss of trees and vegetation may change the rate of evapotranspiration, increasing soil moisture and overall water yield. A short term (5-10 years) increase in discharge during the wet and the dry periods could occur from two mechanisms for the thinned stands: increased snow accumulation (wet period) will create small increases in peak flows (Jones and Grant 1996), and reduced canopy (dry periods) will reduce transpiration rates which will account for small increases in summer flows.

Peak (and pulsed) stream flows occur regularly in the action area, normally from November to April, and carry the majority of the sediment load with high stream energies that may erode and change channel form (Cooper 2005).

Tree harvest and new permanent and temporary road construction do have potential to change the peak and base flows by decreasing the current canopy cover within the treated stands. The causal effect of road maintenance or reconstruction for peak/baseflows are due to water withdrawals, dewatering of a work site, or water pumping to a different location. These activities can last less than an hour for dust abatement to a few days for culvert replacement.

The causal effect of road building for peak/baseflows creates the largest effects to peak/baseflows of any other activity in this consultation. These effects will last as long as the road is in place.

Temporary road removal will re-establish the natural drainage patterns to the streams resulting in a beneficial effect. Temporary road removal will re-vegetate over time resulting in an intact canopy and a reduction in openings.  Temporary roads will be decommissioned at the end of the project. Although this increase in canopy cover on decommissioned roads is positive, at the watershed scale, the road area is small and the creation of temporary roads will offset the canopy closures, thus is discountable in terms of the potential to affect peak and base flows.

Water withdrawals have direct effects to peak/base flow. Drafting from streams may occur during spring, summer and fall, which encompasses the base flow period when the impact locally will be greatest, but will be isolated in space and time in that multiple drafting operations is unlikely to occur simultaneously in the action area. Stream flows may be temporarily reduced downstream from the withdrawal point. The amount of flow decrease from water withdrawals would depend on the amount of stream flow, how much water is withdrawn, and the duration of water drafting. Water trucks commonly hold 500 gallons of water, with a withdrawal rate of up to approximately 7.5 gal/sec. This means the maximum withdrawal would last at least 67 sec, and the maximum withdrawal period commonly is less than 5 minutes at lower rates of withdrawal. The magnitude of flow reductions will be insignificant due to the high stream flow volume available and the short period of withdrawal.

Water withdrawal for road work typically occurs during the dry season (generally June to October). While water quantity to LFH may be temporarily reduced for short periods of time, the magnitude of flow reductions would be insignificant to affect the species or quality of their critical habitat because the periods of withdrawal will be short and would not affect inflow from adjacent tributary streams and hyporheic flow downstream from the point of withdrawal.

The implementation of future road construction or timber harvest activities would result mostly in insignificant overall effects to peak and base flows, mainly through drafting of surface water for fuels treatment (during peak flow from fall through spring), and road maintenance (during base flow in summer and early fall). However, future projects may create small significant effects to peak and base flows, it would depend on the specifics of the project.

Vegetation changes would result in short term reduced evapotranspiration, increasing soil water, and therefore increasing yield. Road work will reduce soil water infiltration affecting storage, delivery and timing of flow to stream channels.

Overall, activities will have small measurable effects on salmonids and their critical habitat.

### Riparian Resilience

Riparian resilience is related to the effects and processes of aquatic management zone to move toward more desirable conditions. Typically, treatments within the aquatic management zone area designed to maintain or improve riparian conditions even though some short-term local impacts are possible. These short-term impacts include a potential slight loss of large wood recruitment from treated riparian stands, potential short-term sediment, and potential minor increase in stream temperature, resulting in a short-term negative effect to this indicator at the local scale. In the long-term the effect would be positive as riparian stand diversity and diameters increase. Utilizing a stream protection buffer determined by the Aquatic Conservation Strategy or the Land Management Plan minimizes short term effects. In the long term, riparian resilience may improve due to benefits expected with future project development.

Timber felling and site restoration can have direct short-term negative effects and long-term beneficial effects, depending on the prescribed treatment within the aquatic management zones. Timber thinning prescriptions within the aquatic management zones can provide long-term beneficial effects by reducing competition between trees allowing trees left to grow and provide future large woody debris. Effects from timber treatments in the aquatic management zone come with short-term negative effects as well just because it takes time for causal mechanisms to improve. Forest thinning remains one of many forest management tools employed to meet ecological, social, and economic objectives on Forests. Thinning within the aquatic management zones is expected to result in a closed canopy within 3-5 years. The temporal boundary for cumulative effects on aquatic species is 5 years. Timber harvest projects within aquatic management zones are expected to be low and the objectives would be to improve the condition of the riparian areas, which would provide for long-term beneficial effects.

Timber yarding and landings have short-term negative effects through compaction of soil, which would take time to rebound. There are measures that are typically used during timber yarding to minimize soil compaction depending on what types of yarding will be utilized. This can range from utilizing slash for heavy equipment to drive over, to skyline yarding. There is no way to predict what type of yarding practices will be utilized for future projects, but we do know any future project will need ESA consultation, and all previous consultations contain project designs that minimize effects to species and critical habitats. The expectation for effects would still be measurable low-level effects to species and critical habitat for these actions.

There are no causal effects of road maintenance or reconstruction and permanent and temporary road construction for riparian resilience, and the effects of road maintenance or reconstruction would be neutral to this element.

There are no causal effects of permanent and temporary road construction for riparian resilience, and the effects of permanent and temporary road construction would be neutral to this element.

Temporary road removal will have long-term beneficial effects for riparian resilience by removing the road prism, soil decompaction, and recontouring the landscape within the aquatic management zone.

Overall, activities will have small measurable negative effects and small measurable beneficial effects to salmonids and their critical habitat.

### Road Density/Location

Road density/location is based on where new permanent roads and temporary roads will be built. Almost all forest watersheds in the action area have existing primary and secondary road systems in place. Current road systems need maintenance and repairs over time to maintain safety standards. Any effects to salmonids and their critical habitat occur on roads that are hydrologically connected to LFH or where effects can reach LFH. The probability of new roads being built somewhere in the action area is high, it would be more likely to occur where the lands are operable and land management plans allow. The probability of the new road being a permanent road is relatively low, as new permanent roads need funding to build and maintain. Temporary roads are associated with timber harvest needs. If timber is harvested in an area, temporary road will be built.

There are no causal effects of timber harvest for road density/location, and the effects of timber harvest would be neutral to this element.

There are no causal effects of road maintenance or reconstruction for road density/location, and the effects of road maintenance or reconstruction would be neutral to this element.

Sediment and turbidity tend to be the most frequent effects associated with timber harvest tasks, road maintenance and reconstruction and road construction. The potential for surface erosion is directly related to the amount of bare compacted soil exposed to rainfall and runoff and timber road haul; hence timber harvest activities may contribute large quantities of fine sediment to streams (Chamberlin et al. 1991, Swanston and Swanson 1976). Not all hillside erosion reaches stream channels (Chamberlin et al. 1991, Rice et al. 1972), but the potential exists for negative effects to the sedimentation/turbidity pathway. Heavy equipment can cause compaction and rutting of the ground which aids the movement of sediment- laden runoff. Excessive erosion may occur on poorly designed and placed skid trails, skid trails used during wet conditions, and trails that have connection to stream channels. Soil compaction from ground-based systems can be reduced when this equipment is restricted to dry soil conditions (Sidle and Drlica 1981). Fine sediment deposited in spawning gravels can reduce egg survival and developing alevins (Everest et al. 1987; Hicks et al. 1991) by reducing the availability of dissolved oxygen in the gravel.

The capacity of headwater streams to retain sediment is supported by many studies. The channels contain vegetation and woody debris of varying amounts, much of it small in diameter but in these small streams this material acts as an important obstruction to store sediment (Sullivan et al. 1987). Some studies indicate that annual sediment yields from small watershed make up less than ten percent of the sediment stored in channels (Swanson and Lienkaemper 1978), in other words, headwater stream channels with have a tremendous capability to store large amounts of fine sediment (Benda et al. 2005).

Chase et al. (2019) completed a soil and streamside effects study on 30 harvest units across Western Oregon and Washington on Weyerhaueser ownership on slopes up to 160%; with most 0-60%. Timing of clear-cut harvest was during the wet season (November – March). They observed that streams with riparian buffers >15m (49’) wide had little to no stream-adjacent disturbance.

Broderson (1973, cited in Castelle et al 1992) found that 50-foot buffers were sufficient for controlling most sedimentation on less than 50% slopes, while steeper slopes required wider buffers. Broderson noted that buffers have little or no effect on sediment removal if sedimentladen water flows across the buffers as channelized flow; buffers can be effective only if they resist channelization and maintain overland flow as sheetflow. A maximum buffer width of 200 feet was found to be effective even on extremely steep slopes.

## Eulachon

The eulachon is a smelt in the Family Osmeridae. The Genus Thaleichthys has only one species. Eulachon are an anadromous fish, meaning adults spend most of their life in the ocean but migrate into fresh water to spawn. Overall eulachon are endemic to the northeastern Pacific Ocean, ranging from northern California to the southeastern Bering Sea in Bristol Bay, Alaska (Willson et al. 2006). This distribution coincides closely with the distribution of the coastal temperate rain forest ecosystem on the west coast of North America (except for populations spawning west of Cook Inlet Alaska). On March 18, 2010, the southern Distinct Population Segment of Eulachon were listed as threatened under the Endangered Species Act (ESA) (75 FR 13012). The southern DPS consists of populations spawning in rivers south of the Nass River in British Columbia, Canada, to, and including, the Mad River in California (NMFS 2011a). Detailed information about the biology, habitat, and conservation status and trends of eulachon can be found in the listing regulations and critical habitat designations published in the Federal Register (75 FR 13012), as well as in recovery plans and other sources (NMFS 2011a; NMFS 2011b).

Eulachon spend 95 to 98 percent of their lives at sea (Hay and McCarter, 2000), little is known of their life history for their saltwater life stage (NMFS 2011a). Spawning generally occurs in January, February, and March in the Columbia River and the coastal rivers of Washington and Oregon (NMFS 2011b). Entry into the spawning rivers appears to be related to water temperature and the occurrence of high tides (NMFS 2011a). Eulachon lay their eggs in freshwater where the eggs stick to the substrate until they hatch 20-40 days later. The larvae are carried to the estuary/ocean by the flow of the natal creek or river. It is not known how long larval eulachon remain in the estuary before entering the ocean. Similar to salmon, juvenile eulachon are thought to imprint on the chemical signature of their natal river basins (NMFS 2011a). Once juvenile eulachon enter the ocean, they move from shallow nearshore areas to deeper areas over the continental shelf. Larvae and young juveniles become widely distributed in coastal waters, where they are typically found near the ocean bottom in waters 20-180 m deep. Little information is available about eulachon movements in nearshore marine areas and the open ocean (NMFS 2011a).

In the portion of the species’ range that lies south of the U.S.–Canada border, most eulachon production originates in the Columbia River Basin. Within the Columbia River Basin, the major and most consistent spawning runs return to the mainstem of the Columbia River and the Cowlitz River (Gustafson et al. 2010). Spawning also occurs in other tributaries to the Columbia River, including the Grays, Elochoman, Kalama, Lewis, and Sandy rivers (WDFW and ODFW 2001). Eulachon also spawn in the lower portions of the Elwha and Quinault rivers in Washington (NMFS 2011b). Little is known about southern DPS of eulachon compared to salmonids, although the distribution and biology of this species is similar to salmon and steelhead.

NMFS (2011c) identified the following Physical and Biological Features essential to conservation of eulachon as:

1. Freshwater spawning and incubation sites with water flow, quality and temperature conditions and substrate supporting spawning and incubation, and with migratory access for adults and juveniles. These features are essential to conservation because without them the species cannot successfully spawn and produce offspring.

2. Freshwater and estuarine migration corridors associated with spawning and incubation sites that are free of obstruction and with water flow, quality and temperature conditions supporting larval and adult mobility, and with abundant prey items supporting larval feeding after the yolk sac is depleted. These features are essential to conservation because they allow adult fish to swim upstream to reach spawning areas and they allow larval fish to proceed downstream and reach the ocean.

3. Nearshore and offshore marine foraging habitat with water quality and available prey, supporting juveniles and adult survival. Eulachon prey on a wide variety of species including crustaceans such as copepods and euphausiids, unidentified malacostracans, cumaceans, mysids, barnacle larvae, and worm larvae. These features are essential to conservation because they allow juvenile fish to survive, grow, and reach maturity, and they allow adult fish to survive and return to freshwater systems to spawn.

When NMFS listed eulachon as a threatened species, they identified a number of activities that may affect the physical and biological features essential to the southern DPS of eulachon such that special management considerations or protection may be required. This information is found in Table 7 and relates to dredging activities during eulachon spawning season. Future projects within the affected environment would not meet this consideration.

## Green Sturgeon, Southern DPS

Two DPSs have been defined for green sturgeon: a northern DPS (spawning populations in the Klamath and Rogue rivers) and a southern DPS (spawners in the Sacramento River). The southern DPS (sDPS) of green sturgeon was listed as threatened in 2006 and includes all naturally spawned populations of green sturgeon that occur south of the Eel River in Humboldt County, California (71 FR 17757). When not spawning, this anadromous species is broadly distributed in nearshore marine areas from Mexico to the Bering Sea. Although it is commonly observed in bays, estuaries, and sometimes the deep riverine mainstem in lower elevation reaches of non-natal rivers along the west coast of North America, the distribution and timing of estuarine use are poorly understood. The principal factor for the decline of southern green sturgeon is the reduction of its spawning area to a single known population limited to a small portion of the Sacramento River. It is currently at risk of extinction primarily because of humaninduced ‘‘takes’’ involving elimination of freshwater spawning habitat, degradation of freshwater and estuarine habitat quality, water diversions, fishing, and other causes (NMFS 2018). Adequate water flow and temperature are issues of concern. Water diversions pose an unknown but potentially serious threat within the Sacramento and Feather Rivers and the Sacramento River Delta. Poaching also poses an unknown but potentially serious threat because of high demand for sturgeon caviar. The effects of contaminants and nonnative species are also unknown but potentially serious threats. Retention of southern green sturgeon in both recreational and commercial fisheries is now prohibited within the western states, but the effect of capture/release in these fisheries is unknown. Climate change is likely to reduce the conservation value of designated critical habitats in the Pacific Northwest. Limiting factors outlined in the recovery plan (NMFS 2018) lists four factors: reduction in spawning area to a single population, lack of water quantity, poor water quality, and poaching (Table 7). Detailed information on the natural history of green sturgeon is provided in the proposed rule to designate critical habitat (73 FR 52084; September 8, 2008) and in the final biological report (NMFS 2009a) prepared in support of this final rule.

## Killer Whale

Southern resident killer whales spend several months of the summer and fall each year in the inland waters of the Salish Sea including Washington’s Puget Sound. The southern resident killer whale population has fluctuated considerably since the 1970’s. The population now numbers in the 70s. When in the Salish Sea, southern resident killer whale prey primarily on adult Chinook salmon returning to the Fraser River in Canada (NMFS 2016i). During summer forays to the outer coast the whales may also consume salmon from other stocks, such as from the Columbia River system. Southern resident killer whales have been known to travel to Southeast Alaska to Monterey Bay in California preying on salmonids (NMFS 2016i). Overall studies have shown that their prey base includes 22 species of fish and one species of squid (Ford et al. 1998; Ford and Ellis 2006). Their late summer and fall diet is more of a mystery, but data indicates that it includes coho salmon in late summer and Puget Sound Chinook and chum salmon in fall (NMFS 2021a). Detailed information about the biology, habitat, and conservation status and trends of southern resident killer whale can be found in the listing regulations and critical habitat designations published in the Federal Register (70 FR 69903), as well as in the recovery plans and other sources.

NMFS (2021a) identified the following physical and biological features essential to conservation of southern resident killer whale (essential features):

1. Water quality to support growth and development;

2. Prey species of sufficient quantity, quality, and availability to support individual growth, reproduction, and development, as well as overall population growth; and

3. Passage conditions to allow for migration, resting, and foraging.

Researchers are unsure about which threats are most significant to the southern resident killer whale population, and none of the threats have been identified as the cause of the recent decline of the population (Krahn et al. 2002). There is limited information on how these factors or additional unknown factors may be affecting southern resident killer whale in coastal waters in winter. It is possible that two or more of these factors may act together to harm the whales. The small size of the population increases the level of concern for all these risks (NMFS 2008).

Water quality concerns are focused on biomagnification of pollutants, such as chemical pollution from industrial waste and oil spills. The contaminants are concentrated near areas of high human population and industrialization or sedimentation and affect southern resident killer whale typically through trophic relationships they have with their prey base.

Quantity and quality of prey availability is identified as a major threat to the survival of southern resident killer whale (PFMC 2020). Salmon, particularly Chinook salmon, are the preferred prey of southern resident killer whale in inland waters of Washington State during spring, summer, and early fall which is most likely due to the species’ large size, high fat and energy content, and year-round occurrence in the area (Baird and Hanson 2004; Hauser 2006). Killer whales also captured older (larger) than average Chinook salmon (Ford and Ellis 2006). When prey abundance is low, southern resident killer whale may spend more time and energy foraging than when prey abundance is high; potential consequences for fitness include reduced reproductive rates and higher mortality rates. Ford and Ellis (2006) correlated coast-wide reduction in Chinook abundance (Alaska, British Columbia, and Washington) with decreased survival of resident whales (Northern and Southern Residents), but changes in killer whale abundance have not been linked to local areas or changes in salmon stock groups. No recent changes in salmon populations are obviously apparent that might be responsible for the recent decline in the killer whale population between 1996 and 2001 (NMFS 2008).

Past peer reviewed information indicated salmon are the preferred prey of southern resident killer whale year-round (Krahn et al. 2002, 2004), including while in coastal waters, and that Chinook are the preferred salmon species (Ford and Ellis 2006). Prey abundance is a concern for killer whales, both near-term and long-term. To survive in the near term, southern resident killer whale require regular supplies of adult Chinook prey in the ocean. To recover over the longer term, southern resident killer whale require abundant Chinook stocks coast-wide, likely including stocks from the Columbia River (Couture et al. 2022).

Although there is currently no quantitative model that identifies a low abundance that will definitively cause adverse effects to the whales or appreciably alter the value of their habitat, there is evidence southern resident killer whale and other killer whale populations that are known to consume Chinook salmon may have experienced adverse effects from low prey availability in the late 1990s (NMFS 2021a). In recent years, peer reviewed information found the relationship between Chinook salmon abundance and SRKW demographic rates has weakened, as southern resident killer whale status has declined despite varying levels of Chinook abundance (NMFS 2021a, 2021b; PFMC 2020). There are several challenges to quantitatively characterize the relationship between southern resident killer whale and Chinook salmon, including (1) there are multiple, interacting factors at play; (2) the strength of any one effect likely varies through time leading to a situation known as “non-stationarity”; and (3) the small population size of the southern resident killer whale population makes detection of any true effects difficult, using statistical analyses, due to a high degree of random variation (NMFS 2021a). Multiple threats affect southern resident killer whale demographic performance through time, in addition to random chance, and these effects can confound the analysis of the effects of prey abundance (NMFS 2021a).

All projects must be consistent with the forest land management plan.

# CONCLUSION

Rescinding the 2001 Roadless Rule will increase the likelihood of road construction and timber harvests occurring in locations previously considered inventoried roadless areas. In many cases the Forest Service anticipates this increased activity to adversely affect ESA-listed species and their designated critical habitats. However, all future projects in these areas will be required to comply with environmental law, regulation, and policy, including the Endangered Species Act. Projects that are determined that they “may affect” ESA-listed species or designated critical habitats will undergo consultation under section 7(a)(2) of the ESA. Through consultation with the Services at the project level, jeopardizing listed species or adversely modifying critical habitat should be avoided.

## Appendix C. Definitions

**Road:** A motor vehicle travelway over 50 inches wide, unless designated and managed as a trail. A road may be classified, unclassified, or temporary.

(1) **Classified road:** A road wholly or partially within or adjacent to National Forest System lands that is determined to be needed for long-term motor vehicle access, including State roads, county roads, privately owned roads, National Forest System roads, and other roads authorized by the Forest Service.

(2) **Unclassified road:** A road on National Forest System lands that is not managed as part of the forest transportation system, such as unplanned roads, abandoned travelways, and off-road vehicle tracks that have not been designated and managed as a trail; and those roads that were once under permit or other authorization and were not decommissioned upon the termination of the authorization.

(3) **Temporary road.** A road authorized by contract, permit, lease, other written authorization, or emergency operation, not intended to be part of the forest transportation system and not necessary for long-term resource management.

**Road construction:** Activity that results in the addition of forest classified or temporary road miles.

**Road maintenance:** The ongoing upkeep of a road necessary to retain or restore the road to the approved road management objective.

**Road reconstruction:** Activity that results in improvement or realignment of an existing classified road defined as follows:

- (1) **Road improvement:** Activity that results in an increase of an existing road's traffic service level, expansion of its capacity, or a change in its original design function.

- (2) **Road realignment:** Activity that results in a new location of an existing road or portions of an existing road, and treatment of the old roadway.
